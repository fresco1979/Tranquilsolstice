Work Hard — And Then Die?

Fresco Su · September 2026 · 5 min read · Also in 繁體中文

One night in 2025, while doomscrolling on social media, I came across a print and, having very little willpower, ordered it on the spot. It was Work Hard — And Then Die (glow-in-the-dark edition), a limited global release. More importantly, it was by Joan Cornellà, the Spanish contemporary artist I've long admired.

The characters in Cornellà's work are always smiling. It's a social smile so deeply internalised that not even death can wipe it off. No matter how cruel the scene unfolding before them, they meet it with the same fixed grin, like the protagonist of George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four, hollowed out and left with no choice, arriving at that final line: "He loved Big Brother."

I've always been a believer in dark humour. And "life is short" has become my deepest lesson from the major shifts in my work and life over the past few years. If life is this short, why take it so seriously?

For the modern wage slave grinding through each day, this piece is the perfect reminder. We all work hard, try to please others, and build our sense of self-worth. We work ourselves to the bone, chasing titles, chasing success. Some of us make it. Some of us lose our way. Some of us lose our health.

Last year, I left a corporate career of nearly twenty years. Throughout that time, I kept searching for an answer to one question. Replying to the boss's messages at 11 p.m. Preparing slides for the CEO on a Sunday. Drinking with clients, toasting like brothers while saying nothing of substance. A whole year of hard work, then another year, and another. Negotiating performance reviews with HR and an ever-changing line of managers, all to secure an extra 5% on a bonus or a raise, in the hope of a better title, a bigger team, more challenges, of becoming a better "leader." These scenes play out again and again in every country and every work culture, happening to every wage slave who looks very, very busy.

Then twenty, thirty years go by. We have savings, property, a European car. And then what?

This is the conversation I most love having with friends. It rarely has an answer, and it never really ends, because everyone works for different reasons and with a different sense of purpose, and everyone works hard in their own way. But we all share one question: is there a better way to find more fulfilment and joy outside of work? And that question is tightly bound up with financial security and how we think about money.

Many people are extremely good at making money, and they know how to invest what they've worked so hard for, in property and in stocks. But ask them: do these investments actually make you feel secure? If you lost your job, fell ill, or were hit by a black swan in the markets, could you still hold on to what you've built? If you decided to retire, what is your magic number? Do you truly understand the quantitative relationship within the magic triangle of your wealth, the meaning of your life, and how hard you work?

This is why I'm so passionate about wealth advisory. Financial planning sounds like it's just about managing numbers and investments, but the real motivations underneath are completely different for every person and every family. There is no standard answer. And that is exactly what makes wealth management so fascinating.

When it comes to wealth management, most people's first question is: what should I buy? Nvidia or Apple? But here's what I'd like to share with you: financial planning is not about chasing numbers or hunting for the next ten-bagger. It is about anchoring your life's purpose and goals with real clarity, so that your wealth works for you, rather than the other way around, where you end up held hostage by it.

So don't just work hard and then die. Let's work hard, truly live, and move towards the goals that matter in our lives.
