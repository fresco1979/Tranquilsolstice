Getting rich and staying rich: two opposite skills

Fresco Su · October 2026 · 7 min read · Also in 繁體中文

In every era, some people get rich overnight, and some people are back to square one overnight. The difference between them often isn't who is smarter or luckier, but whether they understand this: making money and keeping money rely on two completely different sets of skills. In each of the four stories below, you can see this stark contrast.

1. Bill Hwang vs. Bill Gates: two Bills, two endings

Korean-American investor Bill Hwang set up his family office, Archegos, in 2013 with about US$200 million, and within a few short years grew his fortune to around US$20 billion. His method was to concentrate his capital in a handful of stocks, then use derivative contracts to amplify it to more than five times leverage. In March 2021, Viacom, one of his heaviest bets, announced a share offering and its stock fell more than 20%. Under five-times leverage, in just two days, he lost about US$20 billion.

Gates took the completely opposite path. Before Microsoft listed in 1986, he held about 49% of its shares. Over four decades he has kept selling and giving away his stake, deliberately reducing his dependence on Microsoft. As early as 1994, while Microsoft was still in its high-growth years, he hired Michael Larson to run his personal investment company, Cascade, specifically to diversify his wealth. Larson has said that his strategy was, to a large extent, designed to offset the volatility of Microsoft's share price. Microsoft made Gates rich, but what let him keep that wealth, and still keep giving away tens of billions of dollars, is a structure deliberately built to spread risk.

2. The loyal Enron employee vs. Sylvia Bloom: the same office jobs, completely different endings

At the end of 2000, 62% of the assets in the retirement accounts of employees at the US energy giant Enron were in the company's own stock. One Enron employee had put nearly US$700,000 of retirement savings into Enron stock. When she later testified before Congress, she said she had been proud to invest in her own company, and had entrusted her life savings to its management. In less than a year, Enron's share price fell from over US$80 to under US$1, and her retirement money all but vanished.

Sylvia Bloom, meanwhile, worked as a secretary at a New York law firm for 67 years. She lived in a rent-controlled apartment, took the subway to work and lived a simple life. After she died, her family discovered she had accumulated more than US$9 million, spread across three brokerages and eleven banks, of which US$8.2 million was donated as scholarships for students in need. One office worker put her loyalty to the company and her wealth in the same basket; the other spread her wealth out and quietly kept it.

3. Antoine Walker vs. Junior Bridgeman: the same NBA, opposite scripts

In 1996, 19-year-old Walker was taken sixth overall in the first round by the Boston Celtics. At 21 he signed a six-year, US$71 million contract; he became a three-time All-Star and won the 2006 championship with the Miami Heat. Over a career of more than a decade, his total earnings came to about US$108 million, yet Walker filed for bankruptcy in 2010 and was even forced to sell his championship ring. He later admitted that the money came too fast, and his financial education didn't keep up.

Ulysses Lee Bridgeman entered the NBA in 1975 as the eighth overall pick in the first round, and spent most of his career with the Milwaukee Bucks. His total earnings over a twelve-year career were only about US$3 million. But in the off-seasons he went to work at Wendy's to study the franchise model. After retiring, he started with a single restaurant and at his peak owned more than 450 restaurants. He later moved into Coca-Cola bottling, reaching a net worth of about US$1.4 billion, and in 2024 he even bought a 10% stake in the Bucks. Walker earned more at the start, but it was Bridgeman who kept his wealth in the end. In recent years, more and more star players have begun building investments and networks while still playing. Andre Iguodala, who helped lead the Golden State Warriors to the title and was named 2015 Finals MVP, has partnered with Silicon Valley founders, and in 2017 helped launch the Players Technology Summit. They all understand one thing: talent on the court has a shelf life, but a strategy for keeping wealth, and the action behind it, can last for generations.

4. The Yageo chasers vs. Li Ka-shing: adding more in a frenzy, or diversifying at the top

In 2018, amid a severe shortage of passive components, Yageo hit a record NT$1,310 on 3 July, and many retail investors, urged on by stock-tip gurus, borrowed on margin to chase it at the top. By the end of October, the company's quarterly profit had hit a record high, yet the stock was down to just NT$315.5, and its market value had shrunk by about NT$300 billion in half a year. In 2026 the script played out again: AI servers drove demand for high-end passive components, Japanese and Korean makers' MLCC capacity was close to full, orders spilled over to Taiwanese suppliers, and Yageo raised prices again and again. Its share price climbed from NT$317 in May to a post-split high of NT$1,220 in early July. But in July it corrected sharply, falling back to NT$507, a drop of about 58%. Market analysts attributed this mainly to deleveraging after margin positions overheated.

Li Ka-shing did exactly the opposite. After 1989, he began diversifying his Hong Kong investments into Europe, North America and Australia. From 2013 onwards, he progressively sold assets in mainland China and Hong Kong, realising about HK$176.1 billion in total, and moved into European utilities: water, power and telecommunications. By 2018, only about 10% of CK Hutchison's revenue came from Hong Kong, and through the trade war and social unrest that followed, it became the Hong Kong conglomerate with the least exposure. On succession, he held that a family's wealth should be divided while one is still alive, and arranged separate business territories for his two sons early on. Getting rich depends on reading your era correctly; staying rich depends on being willing to put your eggs in different baskets at the very moment everyone else is optimistic.

Two generations, the same challenge: the capital frenzies of 1990 and 2026

In February 1990, in the era when "Taiwan's money was flooding up to its ankles", the Taiwan Weighted Index surged to 12,682 points. Eight months later it had fallen to 2,485, a drop of nearly 80%, and many "hundred-million households" were margin-called back to square one. Thirty-six years later, Taiwan's market is once again at the centre of the global capital-markets stage: in the first half of 2026 it soared 17,000 points, with the index up nearly 60%. But after this surge, July brought a correction, and many margin investors were forced to cut their losses and get out.

On the other side of the Pacific, a similar drama played out in 2026. In June 2024, Leopold Aschenbrenner, a former OpenAI researcher in his early twenties, published a long essay, Situational Awareness, predicting that AI would reshape the world. A month later, he launched a hedge fund under the same name. According to reports, the fund returned 439% in the first half of 2026, and its assets at one point surged to about US$45 billion. But the fund used around four times leverage. When AI stocks corrected in July, in less than a month its assets fell to about US$10 billion, and it was ultimately forced to sell its listed equity holdings to Citadel at a discount. This time, is it a new era opened up by AI, or another 1990? No one knows. Not even the people who understand AI best.

Precisely because the future cannot be predicted, staying rich has never depended on guessing the direction right. It depends on a structure that keeps you alive whether you guess right or wrong. The stories of these four pairs, plus two Taiwan market cycles thirty-six years apart, all say the same thing: what makes people rich is concentration, leverage and nerve; what keeps people rich is diversification, discipline and a respect for risk. These two abilities point in opposite directions, yet most people, after they have become rich, keep using the same set of skills.

If you have read this far, you may already have seen yourself in one of these stories.

Perhaps, like that Enron employee, you believe deeply in your own company, with most of your net worth tied to a single stock. Perhaps you have just been through an IPO or a round of equity liquidity, received a large payout from your company, or watched your paper wealth swell quickly from investing in tech stocks.

Perhaps you have done nothing wrong. You just haven't had the time to stop and think: if your investments lost half their value from here, how much would your life change?

A structure for keeping wealth is never built after asset prices have already corrected.

As with investing, the best time to start was yesterday; the second-best time is today. The same is true of planning and acting to keep your wealth: now is the best moment you have.
